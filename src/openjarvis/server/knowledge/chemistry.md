# Chemistry

## Chemical Reactions and Equations
Level: 10
Keywords: balancing equations, types of reactions, combination reaction, decomposition reaction, displacement reaction, double displacement, precipitation, exothermic, endothermic, corrosion, rancidity
**Key results:** Mass is conserved, so atoms balance. Combination: $CaO+H_2O\to Ca(OH)_2$ (exothermic). Thermal decomposition: $2FeSO_4\to Fe_2O_3+SO_2+SO_3$; $2Pb(NO_3)_2\to 2PbO+4NO_2+O_2$. Photolysis: $2AgCl\to 2Ag+Cl_2$. Displacement: $Fe+CuSO_4\to FeSO_4+Cu$; a more reactive metal displaces a less reactive one from solution. Double displacement: $Na_2SO_4+BaCl_2\to BaSO_4\downarrow+2NaCl$. Oxidation is gain of O or loss of H; reduction is the reverse. Most decompositions are endothermic; respiration and combustion are exothermic. Corrosion is slow attack on a metal by its surroundings (rust, green coat on copper, black sulphide on silver).
**Methods:** Write correct formulae, then change only coefficients. Balance the element in the fewest species first, O and H usually last; add states and conditions. To classify, compare reactant and product groupings; one reaction can fit two labels (displacement is also redox).
**Common mistakes:** Altering subscripts to balance; naming the oxidising agent as the substance oxidised; calling every precipitation redox; forgetting diatomic $H_2, O_2, Cl_2$. Rancidity is oxidation of fats and oils, slowed by antioxidants, nitrogen flushing and cold storage.
**Teach it:** Ask: "In $CuO+H_2\to Cu+H_2O$, which is the oxidising agent?" Track the oxygen: whoever loses it is reduced and is the oxidiser. Count atoms in a table before and after.

## Acids, Bases and Salts
Level: 10
Keywords: acids and bases, indicators, ph scale, neutralisation, salts, baking soda, washing soda, bleaching powder, plaster of paris, chlor alkali, water of crystallisation
**Key results:** In water acids give $H_3O^+$, soluble bases (alkalis) give $OH^-$. Active metal + dilute acid gives salt + $H_2$ (nitric acid usually does not). Carbonate + acid gives salt + $CO_2$ + water. At 25 °C pH 7 is neutral. Chlor-alkali: $2NaCl+2H_2O\to 2NaOH+Cl_2$ (anode) $+H_2$ (cathode). Bleaching powder: $Ca(OH)_2+Cl_2\to CaOCl_2+H_2O$. Baking soda: $NaCl+H_2O+CO_2+NH_3\to NH_4Cl+NaHCO_3$; heating: $2NaHCO_3\to Na_2CO_3+H_2O+CO_2$. Washing soda: $Na_2CO_3\cdot10H_2O$. Gypsum $CaSO_4\cdot2H_2O$ heated at about 373 K gives plaster $CaSO_4\cdot\frac12H_2O$. Blue vitriol $CuSO_4\cdot5H_2O$ turns white on heating.
**Methods:** Predict salt pH from parents: strong acid + weak base is acidic; weak acid + strong base basic; both strong neutral. Indicators: litmus red in acid, blue in base; phenolphthalein colourless in acid, pink in base; methyl orange red in acid, yellow in base.
**Common mistakes:** Adding water to concentrated acid (always add acid slowly to water); thinking dry HCl gas turns dry litmus red; assuming all salts are neutral; confusing strength with concentration; one pH unit is a tenfold change.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Which is more acidic, pH 2 or pH 4, and by what factor?" Use serial tenfold dilution to make the log scale concrete.

## Metals and Non-metals
Level: 10
Keywords: reactivity series, metals, non-metals, ionic compounds, roasting, calcination, ores, extraction of metals, thermite, alloys, amphoteric oxides, rusting
**Key results:** Series: K > Na > Ca > Mg > Al > Zn > Fe > Pb > (H) > Cu > Hg > Ag > Au. Exceptions: Hg is a liquid metal; Na, K are soft; graphite conducts; diamond is very hard; iodine is lustrous. $Al_2O_3$ and $ZnO$ are amphoteric. Na, K react violently with cold water; Mg with hot water; Al, Zn, Fe with steam. Ionic compounds have high melting points and conduct when molten or dissolved. Aqua regia is 3:1 concentrated HCl : $HNO_3$ and dissolves gold. Alloys: brass (Cu-Zn), bronze (Cu-Sn), solder (Pb-Sn); amalgams contain Hg.
**Methods:** Extraction follows reactivity: top (K to Al) by electrolysis of molten compounds; middle (Zn to Pb) oxide then carbon reduction; low (Hg, some Cu) by heating the ore. Roasting: sulphide heated in excess air. Calcination: carbonate heated in limited air. Thermite: $Fe_2O_3+2Al\to Al_2O_3+2Fe$. Copper refining: impure anode, pure cathode, acidified $CuSO_4$.
**Common mistakes:** Expecting $H_2$ from dilute $HNO_3$ (it oxidises $H_2$; very dilute acid with Mg or Mn is the exception); swapping roasting and calcination; electrolysing aqueous NaCl expecting Na. Rusting needs both water and oxygen.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why can't carbon give aluminium from alumina in ordinary extraction?" Present the series as a tug-of-war for oxygen.

## Carbon and its Compounds
Level: 10
Keywords: covalent bond, carbon compounds, catenation, homologous series, functional groups, ethanol, ethanoic acid, esterification, saponification, soaps and detergents, iupac naming basics
**Key results:** Carbon is tetravalent and catenates. Homologues differ by $CH_2$ (14 u), share chemistry, and show graded physical properties. Alkane $C_nH_{2n+2}$, alkene $C_nH_{2n}$, alkyne $C_nH_{2n-2}$ (open chain, one multiple bond). Alkaline $KMnO_4$ or acidified $K_2Cr_2O_7$ with heat oxidises ethanol to ethanoic acid. Hot conc. $H_2SO_4$ (443 K) dehydrates ethanol to ethene. $2C_2H_5OH+2Na\to 2C_2H_5ONa+H_2$. Ester: $CH_3COOH+C_2H_5OH\rightleftharpoons CH_3COOC_2H_5+H_2O$ (acid catalyst). Vegetable oils hydrogenate over Ni. Under sunlight alkanes undergo substitution with $Cl_2$.
**Methods:** Naming: longest chain containing the functional group, number from the end giving it the lowest locant. Distinguish acid from alcohol with $NaHCO_3$ (acid effervesces). Saturated compounds usually burn with a clean flame, unsaturated ones sooty.
**Common mistakes:** Counting the COOH carbon out of the chain; calling saponification reversible (alkaline hydrolysis is not); soaps form scum with $Ca^{2+}, Mg^{2+}$, detergents largely do not.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why does soap clean oil?" Draw a micelle: hydrocarbon tails buried in grease, ionic heads facing water.

## Mole Concept and Stoichiometry
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: mole concept, avogadro number, molarity, molality, limiting reagent, empirical formula, percentage yield, mole fraction, equivalent weight, stoichiometry, dilution
**Key results:** $n=m/M=N/N_A=V/V_m$ (gases); $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}$; $V_m=22.7$ L at 273.15 K, 1 bar and 22.4 L at 1 atm. Below, $M$ is molarity: $M=n/V_{soln}(L)$; $m=n/m_{solvent}(kg)$; $x_A=n_A/\sum n$. $M=\frac{1000\,d\,w}{M_B}$ with $d$ in g/mL and $w$ the solute mass fraction. Dilution $M_1V_1=M_2V_2$; mixing same solute $M=\sum M_iV_i/\sum V_i$ if volumes add. Equivalents: $N=M\times$ n-factor. Molality and mole fraction are temperature independent.
**Methods:** Balance, convert to moles, divide each by its coefficient; smallest is limiting. Empirical formula: percent to moles, divide by smallest, scale to integers. Sequential reactions: chain mole ratios, multiply yields. Mixtures: set unknown moles, write mass and product equations. Use POAC when one atom is conserved through many steps. Eudiometry: $C_xH_y+(x+y/4)O_2\to xCO_2+\frac y2H_2O$; KOH absorbs $CO_2$.
**Common mistakes:** Comparing masses to find the limiting reagent; solvent vs solution mass; using molar volume for liquid water; rounding 1.5 or 1.33 to an integer; n-factor depends on the reaction.
**Teach it:** Ask: "2 mol $H_2$ and 2 mol $O_2$: what remains?" Use a recipe analogy and an initial-change-final table.

## Atomic Structure
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: bohr model, hydrogen spectrum, quantum numbers, orbitals, de broglie, heisenberg uncertainty, photoelectric effect, electronic configuration, radial nodes, rydberg
**Key results:** For one-electron species: $r_n=52.9\,n^2/Z$ pm, $E_n=-13.6\,Z^2/n^2$ eV, $v\propto Z/n$. $1/\lambda=RZ^2(1/n_1^2-1/n_2^2)$. Lines from level $n$ to ground (many atoms): $n(n-1)/2$. $h\nu=\phi+KE_{max}$. $\lambda=h/p$; electron through $V$ volts: $\lambda\approx\sqrt{150/V}$ Å. $\Delta x\,\Delta p\ge h/4\pi$. Radial nodes $n-l-1$, angular $l$. Orbital angular momentum $\sqrt{l(l+1)}\,\hbar$. In H, subshell energy depends on $n$ only; otherwise on $n+l$ rule (approximate). Cr $3d^54s^1$, Cu $3d^{10}4s^1$.
**Methods:** Fill by aufbau, Pauli, Hund. For cations remove highest $n$ first ($4s$ before $3d$). Spin-only moment $\sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM ($n$ = unpaired electrons). Series limit: set $n_2=\infty$. Radial probability $4\pi r^2R^2$ gives the most probable radius ($a_0$ for H 1s).
**Common mistakes:** Applying Bohr formulas to He neutral; intensity changes photoelectron number, not maximum energy; $m_s$ is not from the Schrödinger solution; $d_{z^2}$ has two conical angular nodes, not planar.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Where is the 1s electron density highest, and where is it most likely found by radius?" Contrast density per volume with shell volume.

## Periodic Table and Periodicity
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: periodic trends, atomic radius, ionisation enthalpy, electron gain enthalpy, electronegativity, effective nuclear charge, diagonal relationship, isoelectronic, lanthanoid contraction
**Key results:** Across a period $Z_{eff}$ rises: radius falls, IE and electronegativity generally rise. Down a group radius rises, IE generally falls. IE exceptions: Be > B, Mg > Al, N > O, P > S. Magnitude of electron gain enthalpy: Cl > F > Br > I; S > O. Noble gases and Be, Mg, N have unfavourable or near-zero values. Isoelectronic: $N^{3-}>O^{2-}>F^->Na^+>Mg^{2+}>Al^{3+}$. Ga radius is slightly below Al (poor 3d shielding). Zr ≈ Hf. Group 13 first IE: B > Tl > Ga > Al > In. Pauling: F 4.0, O 3.5, N and Cl about 3.0. Diagonal pairs: Li/Mg, Be/Al, B/Si. Oxides go basic to acidic across a period; CO, NO, $N_2O$ are neutral.
**Methods:** Compare nuclear charge, shell number, shielding, then subshell stability. A large jump between successive IEs reveals valence electron count. Position: period is highest $n$; d-block group is $ns+(n-1)d$ electrons.
**Common mistakes:** Saying second electron gain of oxygen is exothermic (it is endothermic); treating electronegativity as measurable like IE; ignoring that $IE_2$ order differs (O > F > N > C).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is nitrogen's first IE above oxygen's?" Draw 2p boxes and show the paired-electron repulsion relieved in O.

## Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: vsepr, hybridisation, molecular orbital theory, bond order, dipole moment, lattice energy, fajans rule, hydrogen bonding, resonance, formal charge, molecular shape
**Key results:** Steric number = sigma bonds + lone pairs: 2 sp, 3 $sp^2$, 4 $sp^3$, 5 $sp^3d$, 6 $sp^3d^2$. Repulsion lp-lp > lp-bp > bp-bp. $SF_4$ see-saw, $ClF_3$ T-shaped, $XeF_2$ linear, $XeF_4$ square planar, $XeF_6$ distorted octahedron. Bond order $(N_b-N_a)/2$. Up to $N_2$, $\pi2p$ lies below $\sigma2p_z$; reversed for $O_2,F_2$. $B_2, O_2$ paramagnetic. $\mu$: $NH_3$ > $NF_3$. Fajans: small cation, large anion, high charge increase covalency. Lattice enthalpy magnitude rises with ionic charge and falls with ionic size. Angles: $NH_3$ 107°, $H_2O$ 104.5°; $PH_3$, $H_2S$ near 90°.
**Methods:** Count valence electrons (adjust for charge), draw Lewis structure, minimise formal charge, find steric number; put lone pairs equatorial in trigonal bipyramid. Dipole: add vectors. An odd electron count means paramagnetic; an even count can be too ($B_2, O_2$), so check MO filling. Bent's rule: more electronegative groups take orbitals with less s character (axial in $PCl_xF_y$ for F).
**Common mistakes:** Counting pi bonds in hybridisation; resonance forms are not interconverting; $d\pi$ ideas applied to second period; $o$-nitrophenol H-bonds intramolecularly, so it is more volatile than para.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is $BF_3$ nonpolar but $NF_3$ polar?" Build shapes with balloons, then add bond arrows.

## States of Matter
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: gas laws, ideal gas equation, kinetic theory, rms speed, graham law, van der waals, compressibility factor, critical temperature, partial pressure, vapour pressure, surface tension, viscosity
**Key results:** $PV=nRT$; $PM=dRT$; $p_i=x_iP$. $u_{mp}=\sqrt{2RT/M}$, $u_{av}=\sqrt{8RT/\pi M}$, $u_{rms}=\sqrt{3RT/M}$. Translational KE $=\frac32nRT$. Effusion rate $\propto P/\sqrt{MT}$. $(P+an^2/V^2)(V-nb)=nRT$. $Z=PV_m/RT$: below 1 attractions dominate, above 1 size dominates; $H_2$, He have $Z>1$ near room temperature. $T_c=8a/27Rb$, $P_c=a/27b^2$, $V_c=3b$, $Z_c=3/8$. Boyle temperature $a/Rb$. Higher $T_c$ means easier liquefaction. Liquid surface tension and viscosity fall on heating; gas viscosity rises. Vapour pressure rises with T; boiling occurs when it equals external pressure.
**Methods:** Connected vessels: conserve total moles, equalise pressure. Gas over water: subtract aqueous tension. Maxwell curve: higher $T$ flattens and shifts right, area constant. Limiting cases of van der Waals: low P, $Z=1-a/RTV_m$; high P, $Z=1+Pb/RT$.
**Common mistakes:** Celsius in gas laws; $M$ in g with $R=8.314$ (use kg/mol); $R$ units mismatched (0.0821 L atm, 0.0831 L bar); $b$ is four times actual molecular volume per mole.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Same T: do $H_2$ and $O_2$ have equal speed or kinetic energy?" Relate $\frac12mu^2$ to T.

## Thermodynamics and Thermochemistry
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: enthalpy, entropy, gibbs free energy, first law, hess law, internal energy, isothermal, adiabatic, heat capacity, bond enthalpy, spontaneity, work done by gas
**Key results:** $\Delta U=q+w$, $w=-\int P_{ext}dV$. $\Delta H=\Delta U+\Delta n_gRT$. Ideal gas: $\Delta U=nC_v\Delta T$, $\Delta H=nC_p\Delta T$, $C_p-C_v=R$. Isothermal reversible: $w=-nRT\ln(V_2/V_1)$. Reversible adiabatic: $PV^\gamma$ constant. Free expansion of ideal gas: $q=w=\Delta U=0$, $\Delta S=nR\ln(V_2/V_1)$. $\Delta S=nC_v\ln\frac{T_2}{T_1}+nR\ln\frac{V_2}{V_1}$. $\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$ (constant T); spontaneous at constant T, P if $\Delta G<0$. $\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K$. $\Delta_rH=\sum\Delta_fH(prod)-\sum\Delta_fH(react)$; with bond enthalpies (gases) reactants minus products. Kirchhoff (constant $\Delta C_p$): $\Delta H_2=\Delta H_1+\Delta C_p\Delta T$. Strong acid-strong base neutralisation is about $-57$ kJ per mole of water.
**Methods:** Fix the system and path; use state functions via any convenient reversible path. Elements in standard state have zero $\Delta_fH$. Crossover $T=\Delta H/\Delta S$.
**Common mistakes:** $q/T$ from an irreversible path for $\Delta S$; mixing kJ and J; applying $PV^\gamma$ to an irreversible adiabatic (against constant $P_{ext}$ use $\Delta U=-P_{ext}\Delta V$); confusing $\Delta G^\circ$ with $\Delta G$.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Can an endothermic process be spontaneous?" Melting ice above 273 K shows entropy winning.

## Chemical Equilibrium
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: equilibrium constant, kc and kp, le chatelier, reaction quotient, degree of dissociation, vant hoff equation, inert gas addition, vapour density, equilibrium shift
**Key results:** $K_p=K_c(RT)^{\Delta n_g}$. Reverse gives $1/K$; multiply by $n$ gives $K^n$; adding reactions multiplies $K$. Pure solids and liquids are omitted. $Q<K$ moves forward. $\Delta G=\Delta G^\circ+RT\ln Q$. $\ln\frac{K_2}{K_1}=\frac{\Delta H^\circ}{R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)$ (constant $\Delta H^\circ$). Only temperature changes $K$. Catalyst speeds both directions equally. Inert gas at constant V: no shift; at constant P: shifts toward more gas moles. For $A\to nB$ gaseous: $\alpha=\frac{D-d}{(n-1)d}$, $D$ initial and $d$ equilibrium vapour density. $PCl_5$: $K_p=\alpha^2P/(1-\alpha^2)$.
**Methods:** ICE table in moles or pressures; convert to partial pressure with mole fraction times total P. Approximate small $x$ only when $K$ is very small, then check (under about 5%). Simultaneous equilibria share common species. Large $K$: push to completion, then back off.
**Common mistakes:** Using moles in $K_c$ without dividing by volume; expecting added pure solid to shift equilibrium; saying pressure changes $K$; using atm or arbitrary units in $\Delta G^\circ=-RT\ln K$ (standard states: 1 bar, 1 M).
**Teach it:** Ask: "At equilibrium, have reactions stopped?" Two-way traffic with equal flow; compare $Q$ with $K$ after each disturbance.

## Ionic Equilibrium
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: ph calculation, buffer solution, solubility product, common ion effect, salt hydrolysis, henderson equation, weak acid, titration curve, conjugate acid base, ksp, indicators
**Key results:** $K_w=10^{-14}$ at 298 K (larger when hotter); $K_aK_b=K_w$ for a conjugate pair. Weak acid: $K_a=c\alpha^2/(1-\alpha)$; if small, $[H^+]=\sqrt{K_ac}$. Buffer: $pH=pK_a+\log\frac{[salt]}{[acid]}$, useful within $pK_a\pm1$. Salts: WA-SB $pH=7+\frac12(pK_a+\log c)$; SA-WB $pH=7-\frac12(pK_b+\log c)$; WA-WB $pH=7+\frac12(pK_a-pK_b)$. $A_xB_y$: $K_{sp}=x^xy^ys^{x+y}$. Precipitate when ionic product exceeds $K_{sp}$. Amphiprotic $HA^-$: $pH\approx\frac12(pK_{a1}+pK_{a2})$.
**Methods:** First do stoichiometric neutralisation in moles, then identify what remains: strong, weak, buffer or salt. Very dilute strong acid: include water, $[H^+]=\frac{c+\sqrt{c^2+4K_w}}2$. Half-equivalence gives $pH=pK_a$. Selective precipitation: smallest required ion concentration goes first. Lewis acid accepts a pair ($BF_3$).
**Common mistakes:** $10^{-8}$ M HCl is not pH 8; ignoring dilution on mixing; comparing $K_{sp}$ across different stoichiometries; assuming equivalence is always pH 7 (weak base with strong acid lies below 7).
**Teach it:** Ask: "What is left after mixing 2 mol $CH_3COOH$ with 1 mol NaOH?" Always react first, equilibrate second.

## Redox Reactions
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: oxidation number, balancing redox, half reaction method, disproportionation, oxidising agent, reducing agent, permanganate titration, dichromate, iodometry, n factor
**Key results:** O is usually $-2$ (peroxide $-1$, superoxide $-\frac12$, in $OF_2$ $+2$); H $+1$ ($-1$ in metal hydrides); F always $-1$ in compounds. $CrO_5$: Cr $+6$. $MnO_4^-$: n-factor 5 in acid ($Mn^{2+}$), 3 in neutral or weakly basic ($MnO_2$), 1 in strongly basic ($MnO_4^{2-}$). $Cr_2O_7^{2-}$ acid: 6. $2S_2O_3^{2-}+I_2\to S_4O_6^{2-}+2I^-$. $H_2O_2$ acts both ways. Disproportionation: $Cl_2+2OH^-\to Cl^-+ClO^-+H_2O$ (cold dilute); hot concentrated gives $ClO_3^-$.
**Methods:** Half reactions: balance atoms other than O, H; O with $H_2O$; H with $H^+$; charge with $e^-$; in base add $OH^-$ to cancel $H^+$. Titrations: equivalents equal; back titration subtracts excess. Disproportionation n-factor $=\frac{n_1n_2}{n_1+n_2}$ per mole of reactant. Average oxidation number may hide different atoms (check structure, e.g. $S_2O_3^{2-}$).
**Common mistakes:** HCl or $HNO_3$ to acidify permanganate titrations (use dilute $H_2SO_4$); unbalanced charge; expecting an element in its maximum state to be oxidised further, or in its minimum state to be reduced.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Can $SO_2$ be both oxidant and reductant?" Place S $+4$ on a ladder between $-2$ and $+6$.

## Electrochemistry
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: nernst equation, galvanic cell, electrode potential, conductivity, molar conductivity, kohlrausch law, faraday laws, electrolysis, fuel cell, lead storage battery, corrosion
**Key results:** Oxidation at anode, reduction at cathode (galvanic anode negative; electrolytic positive). $E^\circ_{cell}=E^\circ_c-E^\circ_a$ (reduction values). $E=E^\circ-\frac{0.0591}{n}\log Q$ at 298 K. $\Delta G^\circ=-nFE^\circ=-RT\ln K$. Combine half cells through $\Delta G$: $n_3E_3^\circ=n_1E_1^\circ+n_2E_2^\circ$ when half reactions 1 and 2 add to 3 (a reversed step changes sign). $m=MIt/(nF)$, $F\approx96500$ C/mol. $\kappa=G\times l/A$; $\Lambda_m=1000\kappa/c$ ($\kappa$ in S/cm, c in mol/L). Strong electrolyte: $\Lambda_m=\Lambda^0-A\sqrt c$. $\alpha=\Lambda_m/\Lambda^0$. On dilution $\kappa$ falls, $\Lambda_m$ rises. Lead storage discharge: $Pb+PbO_2+2H_2SO_4\to2PbSO_4+2H_2O$. $H_2$-$O_2$ fuel cell gives water. Rusting is electrochemical (Fe anode, $O_2$ reduced).
**Methods:** Write half reactions to get $n$ and $Q$. Concentration cell: $E^\circ=0$. Electrolysis products: compare discharge potentials and overpotential: aqueous NaCl (concentrated) gives $Cl_2,H_2$; dilute $H_2SO_4$ gives $O_2,H_2$; $CuSO_4$ with Cu anode dissolves anode. Weak electrolyte $\Lambda^0$ by Kohlrausch addition.
**Common mistakes:** Multiplying $E^\circ$ by stoichiometric factors; adding potentials of half cells to get a half cell; salt bridge ions need similar mobility and no precipitation (no KCl with $Ag^+$).
**Teach it:** Ask: "If you double a cell equation, what happens to E and $\Delta G$?" Potential is energy per charge.

## Chemical Kinetics
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: rate law, order of reaction, molecularity, half life, arrhenius equation, activation energy, first order, zero order, rate constant, catalyst, rate determining step
**Key results:** Rate $=-\frac1a\frac{d[A]}{dt}$. Order is experimental; molecularity applies to elementary steps (integer, rarely above 3). Zero: $[A]=[A]_0-kt$, $t_{1/2}=[A]_0/2k$. First: $k=\frac{2.303}t\log\frac{[A]_0}{[A]}$, $t_{1/2}=0.693/k$. For $n\ne1$: $t_{1/2}\propto[A]_0^{1-n}$. $k$ units $(mol/L)^{1-n}s^{-1}$. $k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$; $\log\frac{k_2}{k_1}=\frac{E_a}{2.303R}\left(\frac1{T_1}-\frac1{T_2}\right)$. Catalyst lowers $E_a$, leaves $\Delta H$ and $K$ unchanged. Parallel first order: $k=k_1+k_2$, product ratio $k_1/k_2$. $\Delta H=E_{a,f}-E_{a,b}$. The integrated forms here assume $-d[A]/dt=k[A]^n$; for $aA\to$ products with $k$ defined by the rate above, replace $k$ by $ak$.
**Methods:** Initial rates: change one concentration at a time. Test linear plot: [A], ln[A], 1/[A] vs t. Track first order through pressure, volume of titrant or rotation by expressing [A] from total change. Mechanism: slow step sets rate; remove intermediates using fast pre-equilibrium. Pseudo first order: one reactant in large excess.
**Common mistakes:** Reading order from coefficients; using 0.693/k for other orders; assuming every reaction doubles per 10 K; Celsius in Arrhenius.
**Teach it:** Ask: "For first order, how long until 12.5% of reactant remains?" Count equal half lives, then contrast zero order.

## Solutions and Colligative Properties
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: raoult law, henry law, colligative properties, vant hoff factor, osmotic pressure, freezing point depression, boiling point elevation, azeotrope, molar mass determination, ideal solution
**Key results:** $p_A=x_Ap_A^\circ$; $P=x_Ap_A^\circ+x_Bp_B^\circ$; vapour $y_A=p_A/P$. Henry $p=K_Hx$ (larger $K_H$, lower solubility; rises with T for many gases). Nonvolatile solute: $\frac{p^\circ-p}{p^\circ}=ix_{solute}$ when dilute; exact $\frac{p^\circ-p}{p}=in_B/n_A$. $\Delta T_b=iK_bm$, $\Delta T_f=iK_fm$, $\pi=iCRT$. Water: $K_f=1.86$, $K_b=0.52$ K kg/mol. Dissociation into $n$ particles: $i=1+(n-1)\alpha$; association of $n$: $i=1+(1/n-1)\alpha$. Positive deviation (ethanol-water): minimum boiling azeotrope; negative (water-$HNO_3$, acetone-chloroform): maximum boiling.
**Methods:** Find $i$ first, then molality or molarity. Molar mass from the formula; apparent value is true $M/i$. Compare solutions by $i\times$ concentration. Osmosis: solvent moves toward higher effective concentration; pressure above $\pi$ reverses it.
**Common mistakes:** Solution mass in molality; applying colligative laws to volatile solute; reading a larger $\Delta T_f$ as a higher freezing point; counting 11 particles for $K_4[Fe(CN)_6]$ (5 ions if fully dissociated); forgetting benzoic acid dimerises in benzene.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Which freezes lowest: 0.1 m glucose, NaCl or $CaCl_2$?" Count particles.

## Surface Chemistry
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: adsorption, physisorption, chemisorption, freundlich isotherm, colloids, tyndall effect, coagulation, hardy schulze rule, emulsion, micelle, gold number, catalysis
**Key results:** Adsorption usually has $\Delta H<0$, $\Delta S<0$. Physisorption: weak, reversible, multilayer, falls with T, favours high $T_c$ gases. Chemisorption: specific, monolayer, activation energy, often rises then falls with T. Freundlich $x/m=kP^{1/n}$, $n>1$ in the usual range. Langmuir $\theta=KP/(1+KP)$. Colloid size about 1-1000 nm. Lyophobic sols are easily coagulated. Hardy-Schulze: higher counter-ion charge coagulates better ($Al^{3+}>Ba^{2+}>Na^+$ for negative sols). Hydrated ferric oxide sol (hot water + $FeCl_3$) is positive; $As_2S_3$ negative; AgI takes the sign of the ion in excess. Micelles form above CMC and Kraft temperature. Smaller gold number means better protection. Shape-selective catalyst: zeolite ZSM-5.
**Methods:** Plot $\log(x/m)$ vs $\log P$: slope $1/n$, intercept $\log k$. Coagulating power is inverse of flocculation value. Tyndall needs particles not much smaller than the wavelength and a large refractive index difference.
**Common mistakes:** Confusing absorption with adsorption; picking a coagulating ion with the same charge as the sol (it must be opposite); expecting dialysis to remove colloidal particles (it removes small ions).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why does heating weaken physisorption?" Apply Le Chatelier to an exothermic sticking process.

## p-Block: Groups 13 and 14
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: boron, diborane, borax, boric acid, aluminium chloride, inert pair effect, carbon allotropes, silicones, silicates, carbon monoxide, group 13, group 14
**Key results:** Inert pair: $Tl^+$ and $Pb^{2+}$ more stable than higher states. Lewis acidity $BF_3<BCl_3<BBr_3$ ($p\pi$ back bonding strongest in F). $B_2H_6$: four 2c-2e terminal B-H and two 3c-2e bridges; B is $sp^3$. $B(OH)_3$ is a weak monobasic Lewis acid giving $[B(OH)_4]^-$; polyols such as glycerol raise acidity. Borax $Na_2[B_4O_5(OH)_4]\cdot8H_2O$ is alkaline; bead test gives glassy metaborates. $AlCl_3$ is a dimer (vapour, nonpolar solvents). Catenation C ≫ Si > Ge ≈ Sn. $CCl_4$ resists hydrolysis, $SiCl_4$ hydrolyses (accessible orbitals, larger Si). $CO_2$ is a linear molecule; $SiO_2$ a network. $R_2SiCl_2$ hydrolysis then condensation gives linear silicones; $R_3SiCl$ ends chains, $RSiCl_3$ cross-links. $PbO_2$ is an oxidant. CO: neutral, reducing, ligand, binds haemoglobin strongly. Silicates are built from $SiO_4^{4-}$ tetrahedra sharing O corners.
**Methods:** Predict by size, availability of vacant orbitals, inert pair. Diborane + small amine ($NH_3$, $CH_3NH_2$) cleaves unsymmetrically; bulky $(CH_3)_3N$ symmetrically. $NH_3$ with heat leads to borazine.
**Common mistakes:** Calling boric acid a proton donor; assuming graphite and diamond hybridisation match; writing $PbI_4$ as a stable compound ($Pb^{4+}$ oxidises $I^-$).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is $BF_3$ the weakest Lewis acid of the trihalides?" Sketch the 2p-2p overlap.

## p-Block: Groups 15 and 16
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: nitrogen, ammonia, nitric acid, phosphorus, oxoacids of phosphorus, ozone, sulphuric acid, contact process, haber process, oxides of nitrogen, group 15, group 16
**Key results:** Hydrides: basicity $NH_3>PH_3>AsH_3>SbH_3$; reducing power rises down both groups, as does acidity of group 16 hydrides; boiling points $H_2O>H_2Te>H_2Se>H_2S$; $SbH_3>NH_3>AsH_3>PH_3$ (Bi higher still). $N_2$ unreactive (triple bond); white P reactive (strained $P_4$). N cannot form $NX_5$. $H_3PO_2$ monobasic, two P-H; $H_3PO_3$ dibasic, one P-H; $H_3PO_4$ tribasic. P-H bonds give reducing nature. $PCl_5$ solid is $[PCl_4]^+[PCl_6]^-$. Haber: Fe catalyst with promoters, about 700 K, 200 atm. Ostwald: Pt/Rh. Contact: $V_2O_5$; $SO_3$ absorbed in $H_2SO_4$ to oleum. Cu: dilute $HNO_3$ gives NO, concentrated $NO_2$. Passivity of Fe, Al, Cr in conc. $HNO_3$. $NO_2$ dimerises. $O_3$ is bent, diamagnetic.
**Methods:** For oxoacid basicity count OH groups. Oxide acidity rises with oxidation state. Brown ring: $[Fe(H_2O)_5NO]^{2+}$. Conc. $H_2SO_4$ roles: acid, dehydrating, oxidising.
**Common mistakes:** Calling all H atoms ionisable; giving N a double bond like that in $R_3P=O$ (the $d\pi$-$p\pi$ description is unavailable to N; $R_3N\to O$ is dative); calling $O_2$ diamagnetic.
**Teach it:** Ask: "How many ionisable H in $H_3PO_3$?" Draw structure before counting.

## p-Block: Groups 17 and 18
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: halogens, interhalogen compounds, hydrogen halides, oxoacids of chlorine, bleaching, noble gases, xenon fluorides, xenon oxides, chlorine reactions, group 17, group 18
**Key results:** Bond energy $Cl_2>Br_2>F_2>I_2$. Oxidising power $F_2>Cl_2>Br_2>I_2$. Acid strength HF<HCl<HBr<HI. Boiling point HF>HI>HBr>HCl. Oxoacids $HClO<HClO_2<HClO_3<HClO_4$; $HOCl>HOBr>HOI$. Oxidising ability in solution commonly HClO highest, $HClO_4$ lowest. $2F_2+2H_2O\to4HF+O_2$; $Cl_2+H_2O\rightleftharpoons HCl+HOCl$ (bleaches by oxidation, permanent). Interhalogens $XX'_n$ (n=1,3,5,7) are generally more reactive than halogens except $F_2$. $XeF_2,XeF_4,XeF_6$ from Xe + $F_2$; $XeF_6+3H_2O\to XeO_3+6HF$; $2XeF_2+2H_2O\to2Xe+4HF+O_2$; $6XeF_4+12H_2O\to4Xe+2XeO_3+24HF+3O_2$. Partial hydrolysis of $XeF_6$: $XeOF_4$, $XeO_2F_2$. $XeO_3$ pyramidal, $XeOF_4$ square pyramidal.
**Methods:** Shapes by VSEPR counting lone pairs. Displacement: a higher halogen oxidises a lower halide. Acid strength: bond strength down a group, anion resonance for oxoacids. HBr, HI not prepared with conc. $H_2SO_4$ (oxidised).
**Common mistakes:** Giving F positive oxidation states; assuming HF strong; treating $XeF_6$ hydrolysis as redox (no oxidation state changes).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is HF a weak acid despite F's electronegativity?" Compare H-F bond strength and ion pairing.

## d- and f-Block Elements
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: transition elements, lanthanoids, actinoids, lanthanoid contraction, magnetic moment, potassium dichromate, potassium permanganate, variable oxidation states, coloured ions, interstitial compounds
**Key results:** Zn, Cd, Hg have $d^{10}$ in atom and common ion, so are not typical transition metals. Mn shows +2 to +7. In the 3d series Cu has a positive $E^\circ(M^{2+}/M)$. $Cr^{2+}$ reducing, $Mn^{3+}$ oxidising. $2Cu^+\to Cu^{2+}+Cu$ in water. $2CrO_4^{2-}+2H^+\rightleftharpoons Cr_2O_7^{2-}+H_2O$. Chromite fused with $Na_2CO_3$/air gives chromate. $MnO_2$ + KOH + oxidant gives green $K_2MnO_4$, then oxidation or disproportionation to purple $KMnO_4$. $MnO_4^-$ colour is charge transfer. Lanthanoids mainly +3; $Ce^{4+}$ oxidising, $Eu^{2+},Yb^{2+}$ reducing. Contraction makes Zr/Hf similar; basicity falls La(OH)$_3$ to Lu(OH)$_3$. Actinoids show more states and are radioactive. Interstitial compounds (H, C, N in metal lattices) are hard, high melting, conducting, often non-stoichiometric.
**Methods:** $\mu=\sqrt{n(n+2)}$ BM after removing $4s$ first. Colour: partly filled d (d-d) or charge transfer. Oxide acidity rises with state: MnO basic, $Mn_2O_7$ acidic. Redox product depends on pH: iodide with permanganate gives $I_2$ in acid, $IO_3^-$ in neutral or faintly alkaline.
**Common mistakes:** expecting colour from $d^0$ or $d^{10}$ ions ($Sc^{3+},Ti^{4+},Zn^{2+}$); using spin-only moments for most lanthanoids (orbital contribution); calling the dichromate-chromate change redox.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is $Cr^{2+}$ reducing but $Mn^{3+}$ oxidising, both $d^4$?" Look at the products $d^3$ and $d^5$.

## Coordination Compounds
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: ligands, coordination number, iupac naming complexes, crystal field theory, valence bond theory, spectrochemical series, chelate, werner theory, cfse, complex isomerism, synergic bonding
**Key results:** Series: $I^-<Br^-<Cl^-<F^-<OH^-<C_2O_4^{2-}<H_2O<NH_3<en<NO_2^-<CN^-<CO$. $\Delta_t\approx\frac49\Delta_o$; tetrahedral mostly high spin. CFSE octahedral $(-0.4n_{t2g}+0.6n_{eg})\Delta_o$ plus pairing. $Co^{3+}$ with $NH_3$ is low spin; $[CoF_6]^{3-}$ high spin. $[Ni(CO)_4]$ tetrahedral diamagnetic; $[Ni(CN)_4]^{2-}$ square planar diamagnetic; $[NiCl_4]^{2-}$ tetrahedral paramagnetic. $d^8$ Pt(II), Pd(II) square planar. Chelates are more stable. CO: sigma donation and $\pi$ back donation. Werner: primary valence is oxidation state (ionisable), secondary is coordination number (directional). VBT: $sp^3$ tetrahedral, $dsp^2$ square planar, $d^2sp^3$ inner or $sp^3d^2$ outer octahedral.
**Methods:** Names: cation first; ligands alphabetically (ignore multiplying prefix); anionic complex ends -ate; oxidation state in Roman numerals. Find d count, field strength, filling, then $\mu$ and geometry. Isomers: cis/trans in $MA_4B_2$, square planar $MA_2B_2$; fac/mer in $MA_3B_3$; optical in $M(en)_3$ and cis-$M(en)_2X_2$; linkage ($NO_2$, $SCN$), ionisation, hydrate, coordination. Ions outside the bracket give conductivity and precipitate.
**Common mistakes:** Tetrahedral $MA_2B_2$ has no geometric isomers; treating $EDTA^{4-}$ as monodentate (it is hexadentate); counting ligands instead of donor atoms.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Coordination number of $[Co(en)_3]^{3+}$?" Explain splitting by which d orbitals point at ligands.

## General Organic Chemistry
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: goc, inductive effect, resonance effect, hyperconjugation, carbocation stability, carbanion, free radical, electrophile, nucleophile, acidity order, basicity order, aromaticity
**Key results:** Resonance usually outweighs induction; halogens are a key exception for reactivity ($-I$ beats $+M$, so deactivating), yet $+M$ still directs o,p. Carbocation and radical: $3^\circ>2^\circ>1^\circ>CH_3$; carbanion reverse. Benzyl and allyl stabilised by resonance; lone pair on adjacent O or N strongly stabilises cations. Vinyl and aryl cations are poor. Carbanion stability sp > $sp^2$ > $sp^3$. Aromatic: cyclic, planar, conjugated, $4n+2$ pi electrons; $4n$ planar is antiaromatic. Cyclopentadienyl anion and tropylium are aromatic. Tricyclopropylmethyl cation is exceptionally stable. Ortho-substituted benzoic acids are usually stronger than benzoic acid (ortho effect).
**Methods:** Acidity: stabilise the conjugate base (element, hybridisation, resonance, induction, solvation). Basicity: availability of lone pair. Check aromaticity change first, then resonance, then induction; distance weakens $-I$. $-M$ and $+M$ act at ortho and para, not meta. Count alpha H for hyperconjugation. Resonance structure weight: complete octets, more bonds, less charge separation.
**Common mistakes:** Moving atoms in resonance; counting a lone pair in an $sp^2$ orbital (pyridine) in the ring sextet; ignoring steric inhibition of resonance.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is phenol more acidic than ethanol?" Draw where the negative charge can go.

## Isomerism and Stereochemistry
Level: 11, 12, JEE
Keywords: structural isomers, tautomerism, geometrical isomerism, optical isomerism, chirality, enantiomers, diastereomers, meso compound, r s configuration, e z nomenclature, conformations
**Key results:** Structural: chain, position, functional, metamerism, tautomerism. Chiral means non-superimposable on mirror image; a stereocentre is neither necessary (allenes, hindered biphenyls) nor sufficient (meso). Enantiomers rotate light equally and oppositely; racemate is inactive. With $n$ unlike stereocentres and no symmetry, up to $2^n$ stereoisomers. Tartaric acid has 3: an enantiomeric pair and one meso form. R/S and D/L do not predict rotation sign. Ethane staggered is more stable; butane anti > gauche > partially eclipsed > fully eclipsed. Cyclohexane chair most stable; bulky groups equatorial. Gauche ethylene glycol is favoured by H-bonding.
**Methods:** CIP: atomic number at first point of difference; duplicate multiple bonds. Lowest priority away: clockwise R. In Fischer, lowest on vertical bond: read directly; horizontal: reverse. E/Z by higher priority on each carbon (Z same side). Geometrical isomers need two different groups on each end. Count degree of unsaturation $(2C+2+N-H-X)/2$ first.
**Common mistakes:** Rotating a Fischer projection by 90°; confusing resonance with tautomerism; counting meso twice; cycloalkenes below ring size 8 are effectively only cis.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Can a molecule with two stereocentres be inactive?" Show the internal mirror plane.

## Hydrocarbons
Level: 11, JEE
Keywords: alkanes, alkenes, alkynes, benzene, markovnikov, anti markovnikov, ozonolysis, electrophilic substitution, friedel crafts, directive influence, wurtz reaction, kharasch effect
**Key results:** Wurtz suits symmetrical alkanes. Halogenation selectivity Br > Cl. Alkene $+HX$ follows the more stable carbocation (rearrangement possible); peroxide gives anti-Markovnikov with HBr only. $Br_2$ adds anti. Cold dilute alkaline $KMnO_4$: syn diol; hot acidic cleaves. Ozonolysis with Zn gives aldehydes/ketones. $H_2$/Lindlar: cis alkene; Na/liq. $NH_3$: trans. Alkyne hydration ($Hg^{2+}$, dil. $H_2SO_4$) gives ketone, ethyne gives ethanal. Terminal alkynes react with $NaNH_2$, ammoniacal $Ag^+$ or $Cu^+$. Hot iron tube trimerises ethyne to benzene.
**Methods:** Mechanisms. Radical: initiation, propagation, termination. Addition: $\pi$ attacks $H^+$, then nucleophile; with peroxide, $Br\cdot$ adds first to give the more stable radical. EAS: generate electrophile ($NO_2^+$, $R^+$, $RCO^+$), form arenium ion, lose $H^+$. Activators (OH, $NH_2$, OR, R) o,p; $NO_2$, CN, CHO, COR, COOH, $SO_3H$ meta; halogens o,p but deactivating.
**Common mistakes:** Friedel-Crafts fails on strongly deactivated rings and aniline; alkylation rearranges and over-substitutes (use acylation then reduce); side chain oxidation to COOH needs a benzylic H.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Major product of propene + HBr, with and without peroxide?" Compare which intermediate forms.

## Haloalkanes and Haloarenes
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: sn1, sn2, e1, e2, alkyl halide, aryl halide, grignard reagent, saytzeff rule, nucleophilic substitution, finkelstein, swarts, walden inversion
**Key results:** SN2: one step, inversion, rate $k[RX][Nu]$, methyl > 1° > 2° > 3°, polar aprotic solvent. SN1: carbocation, racemisation (often partial), 3° > 2° > 1°; benzyl, allyl fast; polar protic. Leaving group I > Br > Cl > F. E2 needs anti-periplanar H; strong bulky base and heat favour elimination; Saytzeff alkene usually, Hofmann with bulky base or poor leaving group such as $F^-$ or $NR_3$. Alc. KOH eliminates, aq. KOH substitutes. KCN gives nitrile, AgCN isocyanide; $KNO_2$ mainly nitrite, $AgNO_2$ nitroalkane. Finkelstein NaI/acetone; Swarts AgF. Aryl halides resist substitution (partial double bond, $sp^2$ C); ortho/para $NO_2$ enables addition-elimination. $KNH_2$/liq. $NH_3$ goes via benzyne.
**Methods:** Classify substrate, reagent strength and bulk, solvent, temperature. Watch hydride/alkyl shifts and neighbouring group participation. RMgX in dry ether: with HCHO 1°, other aldehyde 2°, ketone 3° alcohol, $CO_2$ acid (after acidic work-up). $ROH+SOCl_2$ is convenient since by-products $SO_2$, HCl are gases.
**Common mistakes:** Grignard with OH, NH, COOH present (gives RH); expecting fast SN2 from neopentyl halides because they are primary.
**Teach it:** Ask: "2-bromobutane with $OH^-$: what decides the path?" Use a conditions grid.

## Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: alcohol, phenol, ether, lucas test, dehydration, reimer tiemann, kolbe reaction, williamson synthesis, hydroboration, cumene process, picric acid, pcc
**Key results:** Acidity: phenol > water > primary > secondary > tertiary alcohol; nitrophenols stronger. Hydration is Markovnikov; $B_2H_6$ then $H_2O_2/OH^-$ is anti-Markovnikov, syn. PCC: 1° to aldehyde; acidic $KMnO_4$ to acid; Cu at 573 K: 1° aldehyde, 2° ketone, 3° alkene. Lucas (conc. HCl/$ZnCl_2$): 3° at once, 2° about five minutes, 1° not at room temperature. Ethanol with $H_2SO_4$: 413 K ether, 443 K ethene. Phenol: $Br_2$ water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol; $Br_2/CS_2$ cold monobromo. Reimer-Tiemann ($CHCl_3$, NaOH; electrophile $:CCl_2$) gives salicylaldehyde; Kolbe (NaOPh, $CO_2$, then $H^+$) salicylic acid. Cumene + $O_2$ then acid: phenol + acetone.
**Methods:** Williamson: SN2, use less hindered halide with the alkoxide. Dehydration: carbocation, shift, alkene. Ether + HI: cleaved at smaller alkyl by SN2; if a 3° group, SN1 gives 3° iodide; aryl-O bond survives (anisole gives phenol + $CH_3I$).
**Common mistakes:** Phenol does not liberate $CO_2$ from $NaHCO_3$ (picric acid does); tertiary halide with alkoxide gives alkene.
**Teach it:** Ask: "How to make t-butyl methyl ether?" Test both disconnections.

## Aldehydes and Ketones
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: carbonyl compounds, aldol condensation, cannizzaro, nucleophilic addition, tollens test, fehling test, iodoform, clemmensen, wolff kishner, rosenmund, etard, acetal
**Key results:** Addition reactivity: HCHO > aldehydes > ketones; aromatic less than comparable aliphatic. Rosenmund: $RCOCl$, $H_2$/Pd-$BaSO_4$. Stephen: nitrile, $SnCl_2$/HCl, then hydrolysis. DIBAL-H: nitrile or ester to aldehyde. Etard: toluene, $CrO_2Cl_2$, then hydrolysis, gives benzaldehyde. Gattermann-Koch: benzene, CO, HCl, anhydrous $AlCl_3$/CuCl gives benzaldehyde. Tollens: aldehydes (and alpha-hydroxy ketones). Fehling: aliphatic aldehydes, not benzaldehyde. Iodoform: $CH_3CO-$ or $CH_3CH(OH)-$ groups on H or C (includes ethanal, ethanol; not acetic acid or its esters). Clemmensen Zn-Hg/HCl (acid); Wolff-Kishner $N_2H_4$/KOH, glycol (base). $NaBH_4$ reduces aldehydes and ketones, not acids or usual esters.
**Methods:** Aldol (dil. base, alpha H): enolate attacks C=O, protonation, heat dehydrates. Cannizzaro (conc. base, no alpha H): $OH^-$ adds, hydride transfers; crossed with HCHO oxidises HCHO. Acetals: dry HCl, diol protects; aqueous acid removes. Imines form best in mildly acidic pH. Ozonolysis fragments rejoin in intramolecular aldol favouring 5 or 6 rings.
**Common mistakes:** Clemmensen on acid-sensitive substrates; undercounting crossed aldol products (up to four constitutional products from two enolisable aldehydes); bisulphite fails with hindered ketones.
**Teach it:** Ask: "Does 2,2-dimethylpropanal give aldol?" Locate alpha hydrogens first.

## Carboxylic Acids and Derivatives
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: carboxylic acid, acidity of acids, esterification, hell volhard zelinsky, decarboxylation, acid chloride, anhydride, amide, ester hydrolysis, kolbe electrolysis, soda lime
**Key results:** Resonance-equivalent O atoms make carboxylates stable. Electron withdrawing groups raise acidity: $FCH_2COOH>ClCH_2COOH>BrCH_2COOH$; $HCOOH>C_6H_5COOH>CH_3COOH$. Dimers raise boiling points. Prepare by oxidation, nitrile hydrolysis, Grignard + $CO_2$. Derivative reactivity: acyl chloride > anhydride > ester > amide. $PCl_5$, $PCl_3$, $SOCl_2$ give acyl chloride. $LiAlH_4$ or $B_2H_6$ give 1° alcohol; $NaBH_4$ does not. HVZ: $X_2$/red P, then water, alpha halo acid (needs alpha H). Soda lime: $RCOONa\to RH$. Kolbe electrolysis: $R-R$, $CO_2$ at anode, $H_2$ at cathode. Beta-keto and malonic acids decarboxylate on mild heating. Aromatic COOH is meta directing, no Friedel-Crafts.
**Methods:** Fischer esterification: protonate C=O, alcohol adds, proton transfer, water leaves; usually acyl-O cleavage; hindered acids react slowly; drive by removing water. Acyl substitution is addition-elimination. Test: $NaHCO_3$ effervescence. HCOOH also reduces Tollens reagent.
**Common mistakes:** Base hydrolysis gives carboxylate, so needs acidification; assuming $B_2H_6$ attacks esters or nitro groups as readily as COOH (it is selective for the acid).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Rank acetic acid, phenol and ethanol by acidity, with reasons." Compare anion resonance.

## Amines and Diazonium Salts
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: amines, basicity of amines, gabriel synthesis, hoffmann bromamide, carbylamine test, hinsberg test, diazotisation, sandmeyer, azo coupling, aniline
**Key results:** Gas phase basicity 3° > 2° > 1° > $NH_3$. Water: methyl 2° > 1° > 3° > $NH_3$; ethyl 2° > 3° > 1° > $NH_3$. Aniline is much weaker; para donors raise its basicity, withdrawers lower it; ortho substituents usually lower it. Gabriel: 1° aliphatic only. Hoffmann bromamide ($Br_2$/KOH): amide to amine with one carbon less, retention, isocyanate intermediate. $LiAlH_4$: nitrile or amide to amine; ammonolysis gives mixtures. Carbylamine ($CHCl_3$/alc. KOH): only 1° amines. Hinsberg ($C_6H_5SO_2Cl$): 1° product alkali soluble, 2° insoluble, 3° no sulphonamide. Diazotisation 273-278 K. Sandmeyer CuCl/HCl, CuBr/HBr, CuCN/KCN; Gattermann Cu powder; KI gives ArI; $HBF_4$ then heat ArF; warm water phenol; $H_3PO_2$ ArH. Coupling: phenol in mild base, aniline in mild acid, mainly para.
**Methods:** Nitrate or brominate aniline after acetylation to control; hydrolyse later. Direct nitration gives substantial meta (anilinium). Aliphatic 1° amine with $HNO_2$ releases $N_2$, mainly alcohol.
**Common mistakes:** Friedel-Crafts on aniline (Lewis acid binds N); expecting monobromination with $Br_2$ water (gives 2,4,6-tribromoaniline).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is the aqueous order not 3° first?" Balance induction, solvation, crowding.

## Biomolecules
Level: 12, JEE
Keywords: carbohydrates, glucose, anomers, mutarotation, reducing sugar, glycosidic linkage, amino acids, zwitterion, protein structure, dna, vitamins, enzymes
**Key results:** Glucose: HI gives n-hexane; $Br_2$ water gluconic acid; $HNO_3$ saccharic acid; pentaacetate. Open form cannot explain no Schiff test, no bisulphite adduct, and two anomers. Sucrose: $\alpha$-D-glucose C1 to $\beta$-D-fructose C2, non-reducing, hydrolysis inverts rotation. Maltose $\alpha$-1,4; lactose $\beta$-1,4 (galactose-glucose), both reducing. Starch: amylose ($\alpha$-1,4), amylopectin (plus $\alpha$-1,6); cellulose $\beta$-1,4; glycogen highly branched. Fructose reduces Tollens in base by isomerisation. Glycine is achiral; natural amino acids mostly L. $pI$ for neutral amino acid $=(pK_{a1}+pK_{a2})/2$. $\alpha$-helix, $\beta$-sheet held by H bonds; denaturation keeps primary structure. DNA: A=T (2 H bonds), G≡C (3); RNA has uracil, ribose. Fat soluble A, D, E, K. Mutarotation: either glucose anomer equilibrates in water to $+52.7^\circ$. Zwitterion: dipolar, net neutral. Enzymes: specific catalysts, mostly proteins.
**Methods:** $n$ amino acids once each: $n!$ sequences. Peptide of $n$ residues loses $n-1$ water. Acidic or basic side chains: average the two $pK_a$ around the neutral form. Chargaff for duplex DNA only.
**Common mistakes:** calling anomers enantiomers (they differ only at the anomeric carbon: C1 in glucose, C2 in fructose); assuming no water soluble vitamin is stored ($B_{12}$ is).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why is sucrose non-reducing?" Mark both anomeric carbons.

## Practical Chemistry
Level: 11, 12, JEE
Keywords: salt analysis, qualitative analysis, flame test, group reagents, titration, lassaigne test, functional group tests, chromatography, distillation, indicators, brown ring test
**Key results:** Cation groups: I dil. HCl ($Pb^{2+}$); II $H_2S$/HCl ($Cu^{2+},As^{3+}$); III $NH_4Cl/NH_4OH$ ($Fe^{3+},Al^{3+}$); IV $H_2S$/ammoniacal ($Co^{2+},Ni^{2+},Mn^{2+},Zn^{2+}$); V $(NH_4)_2CO_3$ ($Ba^{2+},Sr^{2+},Ca^{2+}$); VI $Mg^{2+}$; $NH_4^+$ tested first with NaOH. Flames: Ca brick red, Sr crimson, Ba apple green, Cu blue-green. Halides with $AgNO_3$: AgCl white, dissolves in $NH_4OH$; AgBr pale yellow, sparingly; AgI yellow, insoluble. Lassaigne: N Prussian blue; S violet with nitroprusside; N+S blood red. Ni with dimethylglyoxime: red. Brown ring (nitrate): fresh $FeSO_4$, then conc. $H_2SO_4$ down the side. Organic tests: phenol with neutral $FeCl_3$ violet; carbonyl with 2,4-DNP yellow to orange-red precipitate; alkene decolourises $Br_2$ water.
**Methods:** HCl lowers $S^{2-}$ in II; $NH_4Cl$ limits $OH^-$ in III. Boil $HNO_3$ in extract before halide test. Titration: $M_1V_1/n_1=M_2V_2/n_2$. Permanganate self-indicates, warm oxalic acid. Strong acid-weak base: methyl orange; weak acid-strong base: phenolphthalein. $R_f$ = spot/solvent distance. Steam distil volatile water-immiscible compounds; reduced pressure for heat-sensitive ones.
**Common mistakes:** before group III, failing to boil off $H_2S$ and then oxidise $Fe^{2+}$ with $HNO_3$; rinsing the conical flask with the solution it will hold; expecting a Lassaigne N test from hydrazine (no C, so no $CN^-$).
**Teach it:** Ask: "Why add $NH_4Cl$ before $NH_4OH$?" Use common ion and $K_{sp}$.
